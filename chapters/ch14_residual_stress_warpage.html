Residual stress, cure shrinkage, and warpage

Handbook anchors for distortion drivers

Cure cycle variations (temperature/pressure/time) affect level of cure and finished thickness, and thickness variations can affect consolidation and curing uniformity. [1] Nonuniform cure and thickness are primary contributors to residual stress gradients and warpage in molded thermoset composites.

14.1 What is “residual stress” in molded thermoset SMC?

Residual stress is internal stress locked into the part after processing, without external load. In SMC compression molding, it is generated mainly by:

  • Cure shrinkage: chemical shrinkage as the polymer network forms.
  • Thermal contraction: cooling from cure temperature to room temperature.
  • Tool constraint and friction: the mold restricts free shrinkage/thermal strain during cure and cooling.
  • Nonuniform cure/temperature: gradients through thickness and across the part create incompatible strains.

14.2 Cure shrinkage vs thermal contraction (why both matter)

In thermoset systems, shrinkage begins during cure (chemical shrinkage) and continues as the part cools. Cure state also governs properties such as stiffness and thermal expansion. [3] This means the same shrinkage strain applied at different cure states creates different stress outcomes.

14.3 Why nonuniformity is the main warpage trigger

14.3.1 Thickness variation leads to cure and consolidation nonuniformity

The handbook excerpt states that thickness variations may affect consolidation and curing uniformity. [1] This is a direct warpage driver:

  • thicker regions heat up and cure differently (Ch06)
  • exotherm effects are more pronounced in thick regions (Ch06) [2]
  • stiffer/earlier-cured regions constrain later-cured regions → stress gradients

14.3.2 Tool temperature nonuniformity creates “built-in bending moments”

If one region of the tool is hotter:

  • that region cures faster → earlier modulus increase
  • chemical shrinkage happens earlier under constraint
  • after demold, the imbalance relaxes as warpage/springback

14.4 Typical distortion modes in SMC parts

  • Global bow / dish in large panels
  • Twist in asymmetric ribs/boss layouts
  • Edge lift due to edge cooling differences and flow-induced orientation gradients
  • Local sink/print-through over ribs (cosmetic + dimensional)

14.5 Process levers to reduce warpage (what actually works)

14.5.1 Improve thermal uniformity and predict thick-part behavior

  • map and control tool temperature field (Ch06)
  • use instrumented trials to capture exotherm peaks for thick parts [2]
  • avoid overdriving cure so rapidly that internal gradients become severe

14.5.2 Reduce thickness variability and improve consolidation uniformity

  • press parallelism and tooling stiffness verification (Ch07)
  • charge mass distribution control (Ch03)
  • stable cure cycle controls to avoid thickness drift [1]

14.5.3 Demold at an appropriate cure state

Demolding too early (part under-cured / too hot / too compliant) can allow distortion during ejection and early cooling. Demolding too late may increase cycle time but can improve dimensional stability. Since cure state affects Tg and stiffness [3], demold criteria should be linked to cure state metrics (Ch05, Ch15).

14.6 Measurement and validation: how to quantify warpage

  • CMM or 3D scanning: compare to nominal CAD; produce deformation maps.
  • Curvature metrics for panels (principal curvature, bow height).
  • Time-dependent checks: measure immediately after demold and after 24–72 hours to see post-cure relaxation.
  • Correlation to process traces: compare warpage to tool temperature field and press golden curves (Ch11).

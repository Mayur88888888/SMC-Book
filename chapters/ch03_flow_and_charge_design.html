Flow and charge design: coverage, placement, knit lines

Why this chapter matters

SMC molding is rarely “just press and cure.” The charge pattern determines how far the material must flow, where flow fronts meet, where air is trapped, and where fiber orientation and resin richness vary. Because viscosity rises as cure progresses, the time available for flow is finite; cure cycle variability can change how much flow occurs before cure and therefore change thickness/consolidation and final quality. [1]

3.1 Flow in compression molding: a practical picture

The dominant kinematic driver is squeeze flow between the closing mold halves. But the effective flow behavior is not that of a neat resin: chopped fibers, fillers, and partially matured resin form a complex material whose apparent viscosity and flow regime depend on temperature and cure state.

3.1.1 Key flow regimes you should expect (engineering taxonomy)

  • Macro-squeeze flow: bulk charge spreads laterally as thickness reduces.
  • Feature filling: ribs/bosses fill through local pressure gradients; can lag behind panel fill.
  • Front meeting / knit line creation: two flow fronts meet and “weld” (strength depends on local conditions).
  • Air evacuation competition: the same paths used to fill must also allow air to escape; venting becomes critical.

3.2 The “charge coverage ratio” concept

Define the coverage ratio as: 

coverage_ratio = (projected area of charge at placement) / (projected area of final part cavity)

Coverage ratio is not a universal constant; it is a design knob trading off:

  • Flow distance and fill difficulty: low coverage requires longer flow and more time in the low-viscosity window.
  • Fiber orientation gradients: long flow distances promote strong alignment along flow paths (anisotropy in properties).
  • Knit line count/locations: multiple charges can create more meeting fronts.
  • Air entrapment risk: “islands” of charge can trap air pockets unless venting and closing profile are well designed.

Heuristic guidance (use as a starting point, then validate)

  • Cosmetic panels: often prefer higher coverage and controlled flow to reduce surface waviness and flow marks.
  • Structural parts: may accept more flow if it improves fiber distribution in features, but knit-line placement must be managed.
  • Electrical/FR parts: often prioritize low porosity and consistent fill; venting strategy becomes high priority.

3.3 Charge placement strategy (how to “design the mold fill”)

3.3.1 Placement objectives

  1. Minimize required flow distance before gel/viscosity rise closes the flow window.
  2. Direct flow to fill hard-to-fill features (ribs/bosses) early enough.
  3. Force flow fronts (knit lines) into low-stress regions or regions that will be trimmed/hidden.
  4. Provide air evacuation paths toward vents and parting lines.

3.3.2 Common placement patterns (single vs multi-charge)

Single central charge

  • Simple handling.
  • Large flow distances to edges/corners; risk of incomplete fill if viscosity rises early.
  • Often produces strong fiber alignment radially outward (anisotropic panel behavior).

Multiple distributed charges

  • Reduces maximum flow distance; helps fill corners and thin features.
  • Creates multiple meeting fronts (knit lines), requiring careful management.
  • Air trapping can worsen if islands of charge seal off pockets before venting.

3.4 Knit lines / weld lines: mechanism and mitigation

Knit lines form when two flow fronts meet. In SMC, this junction can be mechanically weaker due to:

  • fiber orientation mismatch across the meeting plane
  • local resin richness or depletion
  • air/volatile entrapment at the meeting front
  • reduced interdiffusion/“healing” if viscosity is already high due to cure advancement

3.4.1 How cure cycle variability affects knit-line quality

Cure-cycle variations can change resin flow prior to cure and change the time available for fronts to merge under pressure. [1] If the viscosity rises too early, the knit line can become a “frozen” interface with poor consolidation.

3.4.2 Practical mitigation actions

  • Relocate knit lines by changing charge pattern and/or adding flow leaders in tooling.
  • Adjust closing speed profile: allow air to evacuate before sealing and packing.
  • Use venting to prevent a trapped air layer at the meeting front.
  • Stabilize charge temperature and material maturity to reduce viscosity scatter.

3.5 Feature filling: ribs, bosses, and thickness transitions

Features such as ribs and bosses can be “last to fill” because they require local material transport into narrow sections. Feature filling competes with panel filling and can be sensitive to viscosity and pressure history.

3.5.1 Why thickness transitions create problems

  • Local heat-up rates differ; cure advances at different rates across the part thickness.
  • Thickness variation affects consolidation and curing uniformity. [1]
  • Rib roots can create stress concentration and surface sink/print-through risks (cosmetic concern).

3.6 Air evacuation during filling (venting interacts with charge design)

Charge placement defines how air is pushed through the cavity. If a charge island seals off a pocket early, the remaining air must dissolve, compress, or remain as porosity. Since processing conditions affect porosity (time–temperature profile, vacuum/pressure, viscosity/flow), venting and charge design must be treated together. [3]

Design-for-venting principle

Every major flow path should have an air escape path to a vent or parting line before the path is sealed by packing. If you cannot guarantee this with the current charge pattern, change the pattern first before “tuning” the cure cycle.

3.7 A practical workflow for charge design (repeatable method)

  1. Start with geometry: identify hard-to-fill zones (thin ribs, corners, deep draws, bosses).
  2. Propose charge pattern(s): choose coverage ratio and island locations to shorten flow to hard zones.
  3. Predict knit lines: draw expected flow fronts and meeting locations; move them away from critical load paths.
  4. Define vent plan: ensure air evacuation routes exist along the predicted flow and meeting lines.
  5. Trial and instrument: record press force-position-time and mold temperature traces; correlate defects with signatures.
  6. Lock “golden pattern”: standardize charge mass, cut dimensions, and placement tolerance.

3.8 What we quantify next: rheology and the closing flow window

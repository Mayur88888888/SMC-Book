Porosity: mechanisms, venting, and vacuum/pressure scheduling

Handbook-anchored takeaway

Processing parameters such as time–temperature profile, vacuum/pressure, flow rate/viscosity influence porosity level, Tg, and surface finish quality. [3]

8.1 Why porosity is the “universal” SMC defect driver

Porosity (voids, microbubbles, pinholes) can degrade:

  • Cosmetic quality: surface pinholes, paint pop, orange peel, waviness amplification.
  • Structural performance: reduced compressive strength, interlaminar properties, fatigue life; enhanced scatter.
  • Electrical reliability: local dielectric weakness and moisture pathways.

Porosity in SMC molding is not “one mechanism.” It is the net result of (i) gas generation/entrapment, (ii) gas transport/venting, and (iii) void collapse/dissolution before gelation/viscosity rise stops the process.

8.2 Porosity sources (mechanism catalog)

8.2.1 Entrapped air due to charge placement and flow sealing

  • Charge islands can trap air pockets when mold closes (Ch03).
  • Flow fronts can enclose air as they merge (knit lines/weld lines).
  • Premature sealing at parting lines or shutoffs blocks escape paths.

8.2.2 Volatiles and moisture-driven gas generation

  • Moisture can vaporize during heat-up and create internal gas pressure.
  • Low molecular weight components can volatilize depending on temperature and chemistry.
  • Electrical/FR grades may include additives that change volatile behavior; control incoming storage and drying protocols as needed.

8.2.3 Gas expansion due to temperature rise and exotherm

Heat transfer and cure are coupled; in thick parts, exotherm can raise interior temperature significantly, which can increase gas pressure and porosity risk if venting and pressure scheduling are not robust. [2]

8.3 Gas transport and “void removal” pathways

In press molding, gas can be removed by:

  • Venting to atmosphere through vents/parting line
  • Vacuum assistance (when tooling allows and process is designed for it)
  • Compression/collapse of voids under pressure
  • Dissolution/absorption into the liquid phase (chemistry-dependent; often limited in practice as viscosity increases)

SMC-specific reality check

  • Vent paths must remain open long enough to remove air before packing seals the cavity edges.
  • Once viscosity rises (Ch04) and gelation occurs (Ch05), voids are increasingly “frozen in.”

8.4 The porosity process window (flow/viscosity vs pressure)

The handbook excerpt explicitly connects porosity to time–temperature profile, vacuum/pressure, and viscosity/flow. [3] Use the following window concept:

  1. Early: viscosity low → easy flow, easy air transport. Pressure helps collapse voids but may also drive flash if too high.
  2. Mid: filling completes, packing begins. Maintain vents until air removal is complete.
  3. Late: viscosity high / near gel → gas cannot escape; pressure can no longer eliminate all voids effectively.

8.5 Venting design principles (what the tooling must do)

  • Provide distributed vent locations at farthest fill points and around knit-line-prone regions.
  • Prevent vent clogging (resin bleed, filler buildup). Tool maintenance is part of porosity control.
  • Use vent depth/land design that allows air removal without excessive resin loss (flash/bleed).
  • Coordinate with ejection and cosmetics: vents can imprint or create witness marks; plan location carefully.

8.6 Vacuum and pressure scheduling (control logic)

If vacuum is available and compatible with part and tool design, it can reduce initial trapped gas. Pressure scheduling then drives compaction and void collapse.

Generic scheduling strategy (conceptual)

  1. Close to “near-contact” while allowing air to evacuate (vents open).
  2. Apply pressure progressively to promote flow and pack, while ensuring vent paths are not sealed too early.
  3. Full pressure dwell during cure after air removal is complete.

In practice, the correct schedule depends on geometry, charge pattern (Ch03), and viscosity evolution (Ch04).

8.7 Diagnostics: how to tell which porosity mechanism you have

8.7.1 Symptoms and signatures

  • Surface pinholes clustered near far fill points: venting/air removal problem; possibly sealing too early.
  • Blisters after painting or post-heat: volatile/moisture-driven gas expansion; cure state and Tg may be involved.
  • Porosity bands near knit lines: air trapped at meeting fronts; charge placement and closing profile issues.
  • Porosity correlates with thickness: thermal gradient + exotherm + local viscosity rise timing; coupled thermal problem (Ch06, Ch07).

8.7.2 Measurements (from simplest to strongest)

  • Mass and thickness mapping (density proxy)
  • Visual inspection (surface pinholes, cut-and-polish microscopy)
  • Ultrasonic C-scan (if applicable; may be limited for chopped SMC)
  • Micro-CT (best for 3D void mapping, but cost/time)

8.8 Porosity prevention checklist (production control)

  1. Charge control: placement pattern standardized, coverage ratio stable, no contamination.
  2. Tool control: vents clean, parting line conditions verified, release state controlled.
  3. Cycle control: temperature/pressure/time repeatable; remember cure-cycle variations affect flow and finished thickness. [1]
  4. Thermal control: map tool temperatures; thick parts require exotherm awareness. [2]
  5. Demolding discipline: avoid surface/integrity damage during demolding and tool cleaning. [3]

8.9 What’s next: tooling design details

The next chapter formalizes tooling design for SMC: parting lines, vents, flash land, shutoffs, and ejection. These details are essential to implement the venting and air-evacuation principles developed here.

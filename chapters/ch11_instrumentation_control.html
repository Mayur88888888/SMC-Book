Instrumentation and process control: golden curves and control plans

Handbook-anchored principle

Press curing quality depends on meeting cure cycle parameters—pressure, temperature, and time—monitored during the cycle. [4] Processing conditions (time–temperature profile, vacuum/pressure, flow/viscosity) influence porosity, Tg, and surface finish. [3]

11.1 Why “data” is mandatory for robust SMC production

SMC molding outcomes are sensitive to cure and flow coupling:

  • temperature affects viscosity and cure rate (Ch04–Ch06)
  • pressure and close profile affect air removal and void collapse (Ch08–Ch10)
  • thickness variation affects consolidation and cure uniformity (Ch07) [1]

Therefore, robust production requires measuring and controlling the histories that drive these couplings.

11.2 What to measure (minimum viable instrumentation)

11.2.1 Press signals

  • Force (or pressure) vs time
  • Position (or mold gap) vs time
  • Closing speed (derived from position/time)
  • Dwell time and any transitions (venting → packing → cure hold)

11.2.2 Tool temperature signals

  • multiple thermocouples/RTDs per mold half (not just one sensor per platen)
  • zone control readings vs independent verification readings

11.2.3 Product and post-process measurements (feedback and QA)

  • Part mass (fast indicator of charge mass stability and flash loss)
  • Thickness map (links to consolidation and cure uniformity) [1]
  • Visual porosity/pinhole count and surface quality metrics (application-dependent)
  • Tg or cure indicator (sample-based verification for structural/electrical critical parts)

11.3 Process records and traceability

Standard composite manufacturing control practice records curing parameters and critical process steps (e.g., tooling concept, cure cycle, bagging procedures in autoclave contexts). [11] In press molding, the analogous expectation is to record the full cure cycle history and any deviations (temperature/pressure/time). [4]

Recommended “lot traveler” fields for SMC

  • SMC lot ID, storage temperature history, out-time, maturity window status
  • charge mass, pattern ID, placement notes
  • mold ID, mold surface condition/release notes, vent cleaning status
  • tool temperature setpoints and measured readings at cycle start
  • press recipe ID; recorded force-position-time trace; dwell time
  • inspection results: mass, thickness points, surface class metrics, porosity indication

11.4 Control strategies (taxonomy)

Composite process control schemes can be categorized as: empirical methods, active sensor-based control, and passive model-based control. [10]

11.4.1 Empirical (recipe-based) control

  • fixed setpoints for temperature/pressure/time
  • works only if incoming material and tool/press conditions are stable
  • often fails when SMC maturity, vent condition, or tool thermal uniformity drifts

11.4.2 Active sensor-based control

  • adjusts process in real time based on sensor feedback (e.g., temperature, displacement, cure monitoring)
  • can reduce sensitivity to material lot variability and environmental drift
  • requires sensor robustness and well-defined control logic to avoid “chasing noise”

11.4.3 Passive model-based control

  • uses a process model (thermochemical + rheology) to plan or validate the cycle
  • can support recipe generation for new thicknesses/geometries
  • depends on calibrated kinetics and thermal properties (Ch05–Ch06)

11.5 The “golden curve” method (practical and powerful)

A golden curve is a baseline press signature—typically:

  • force vs time
  • position vs time
  • force vs position (often the most diagnostic)

When the same tool and charge pattern are run, deviations in the curve often indicate:

  • material maturity drift: viscosity shifted (Ch04)
  • tool temperature drift: cure rate and viscosity evolution shifted (Ch05–Ch06)
  • vent blockage / air removal issue: early force spikes; later porosity (Ch08–Ch09)
  • press mechanical drift: parallelism/compliance changes; thickness changes (Ch07)

11.5.1 How to implement golden curves

  1. Collect baseline runs under known-good conditions (multiple cycles; stable material lot).
  2. Define metrics (peak force, force at a given position, slope in packing region, area under curve).
  3. Set control limits (SPC-based thresholds).
  4. Link to reaction plans (what to do if a metric drifts: check vents, check tool temperature mapping, check charge mass).

11.6 Reaction plans (turning signals into decisions)

If porosity increases

  • Verify vents are clean and open (Ch09).
  • Check closing profile: venting stage too short? (Ch10).
  • Check tool temperature uniformity; thick parts may have exotherm shifts (Ch06). [2]
  • Check material moisture/volatiles (incoming QC, Ch01).

If thickness drifts

  • Check press parallelism and tool shutoff behavior (Ch07).
  • Check charge mass distribution and placement repeatability (Ch03).
  • Check cure cycle drift (temperature/pressure/time). Cure-cycle variability affects finished thickness. [1]

11.7 Application-specific control emphasis

  • Cosmetic panels: surface metrics + porosity/pinhole monitoring; demolding discipline (Ch12).
  • Structural: porosity limits and thickness/cure uniformity; knit-line management (Ch03, Ch13).
  • Electrical/FR: porosity/dielectric reliability + Tg/cure state validation; additive variability control (Ch01, Ch15).

Press mechanics and cycle design: closing speed, tonnage, dwell

Handbook-anchored control principle

Press curing is monitored to ensure curing parameters are met—specifically pressure, temperature, and time. [4] Cure-cycle variations (temperature, pressure, time) change resin flow prior to cure, the level of cure, and finished thickness, and thickness variation affects consolidation/cure uniformity. [1]

10.1 What the press is “really” controlling

A compression press can be thought of as controlling two coupled histories:

  • Displacement/position history: how the mold gap closes vs time (closing speed profile).
  • Force/pressure history: how much load is applied during filling, packing, and cure dwell.

The material response is governed by temperature and cure state (Ch04–Ch06), so the same press program can yield different outcomes if the charge maturity or tool temperature drifts.

10.2 Tonnage sizing (engineering logic)

Tonnage must be sufficient to:

  • fill features and corners before viscosity rises (flow resistance)
  • compact/consolidate and collapse voids (Ch07–Ch08)
  • maintain shutoff sealing and flash land closure (Ch09)

A common first-pass sizing uses projected area times a target molding pressure, then adds margins for:

  • feature filling pressure spikes
  • tooling compliance and deflection
  • process variability and safety factors

Because you indicated “no limitations,” this book focuses on the physics and control logic rather than a single numeric rule.

10.3 Closing speed profile (the hidden “third parameter”)

Most defects blamed on “temperature” or “pressure” are actually caused by the interaction of closing speed with venting. A well-designed close typically includes:

  1. Approach: fast close to near-contact to reduce cycle time and limit charge cooling.
  2. Venting stage: controlled close allowing air evacuation (vents active; avoid premature sealing).
  3. Packing stage: higher pressure and/or faster close after most air is removed to densify and fill last features.
  4. Dwell: maintain conditions for cure completion (Ch05).

Practical “signature”

In press data, venting-stage effectiveness often appears as a smooth force rise and stable displacement progression. Premature sealing may show as early force spikes combined with later porosity or short-shot symptoms.

10.4 Temperature programming (tool temperature as a boundary condition)

Temperature influences cure kinetics and viscosity evolution; in thick parts, exotherm can significantly affect resin temperature. [2] Therefore:

  • tool temperature uniformity is as important as setpoint value (Ch06)
  • thickness changes alter thermal lag and can change cure state at demold (Ch07)
  • for thick sections, monitor interior temperatures during development to prevent unanticipated temperature peaks (Ch06)

10.5 Dwell design: what “hold time” must accomplish

Dwell time under pressure/temperature must deliver:

  • Void suppression completion: void collapse and gas removal must occur before gel/viscosity rise locks voids in (Ch04–Ch08).
  • Target cure state: sufficient degree of cure/Tg for demolding and performance (Ch05).
  • Thickness stability: avoid post-demold thickness changes and distortion; thickness variations affect consolidation and cure uniformity. [1]

10.6 Cure-cycle variability: why small drifts cause big outcomes

The handbook excerpt states directly that cure-cycle variations affect resin flow prior to cure, cure level, and finished thickness. [1] Here is the engineering translation:

  • Hotter / faster cycle: earlier viscosity rise → shorter fill/vent window → higher porosity/short shot risk; may also increase flash if low viscosity early.
  • Cooler / slower cycle: longer flow window but may under-cure at demold; may need longer dwell to meet Tg and performance.
  • Pressure drift: impacts compaction and void collapse effectiveness; interacts with vent sealing timing.

10.7 Recipe design method (repeatable approach)

  1. Start with a target thermal history consistent with resin kinetics and Tg goals (Ch05–Ch06).
  2. Choose a close profile that prioritizes air removal early (Ch08–Ch09).
  3. Apply packing pressure after air removal to consolidate and fill features (Ch07).
  4. Set dwell to reach demold-safe and performance-safe cure state (Ch05).
  5. Validate with instrumentation (tool temperatures, press curves; Ch11).

10.7.1 Typical press force–displacement signature

Signature shape varies with charge pattern, tooling, and material maturity. Golden curves lock in a known-good baseline (Ch11).

10.8 Documentation and traceability

A disciplined manufacturing system records and maintains the time–temperature–pressure histories for traceability and conformance. This is consistent with standard practice in composite processing control documentation. [10]

Cure kinetics, degree of cure, gelation/vitrification, and Tg evolution

Why cure kinetics belong in a molding handbook

The rate of change of degree of cure is often not linear, which makes it difficult to predict resin response to a new temperature profile without a model. In thick structures, reaction heat can significantly change temperature, which feeds back into degree of cure and viscosity. [8]

5.1 State of cure and why it matters to SMC molding

Many processing and performance properties depend directly on the state of cure. The provided excerpt explicitly lists examples: glass transition temperature, viscosity, cure shrinkage, stiffness, and thermal expansion. [3]

In SMC compression molding this translates to:

  • Flow window control: you must fill/vent before viscosity rises due to cure advancement (Ch04).
  • Dimensional stability: cure shrinkage + thermal contraction + tool constraint drive warpage (Ch14).
  • Demold criteria: insufficient cure can cause distortion or surface damage on ejection (Ch12).

5.2 Degree of cure (DOC) definition (DSC basis)

Degree of cure is commonly defined using the fraction of reaction enthalpy released: 

a(t) = (released heat up to time t) / (total reaction heat)

Practically, you obtain the total heat of reaction from a fully curing DSC experiment and compute instantaneous conversion from partial heat release.

5.3 Cure kinetics: what a “kinetic model” does

A kinetic model aims to predict: 

da/dt = f(a, T)

Key features for thermosets used in SMC:

  • Nonlinear conversion rate: autocatalysis is common; rate does not increase linearly with time. [8]
  • Temperature dependence: rate constants typically follow Arrhenius forms.
  • Coupling with viscosity: as a increases, viscosity rises sharply (Ch04), limiting further flow and changing heat transfer conditions.

5.4 Gelation: the “end of meaningful flow”

Gelation is the transformation of the polymer from a liquid to a rubbery network state. [9] For molding, gelation is a critical boundary:

  • Before gelation: significant redistribution/flow is possible (filling and knit-line healing are most active).
  • Near gelation: viscosity and relaxation time rise; the part begins to lock in residual stresses and flow-induced orientation patterns.
  • After gelation: flow becomes extremely limited; further deformation resembles viscoelastic/solid mechanics rather than fluid mechanics.

5.4.1 Degree of cure at gelation, ag

Viscosity models often incorporate a gel point parameter ag; for example, the excerpted cure-dependent viscosity model explicitly uses ag and diverges as a approaches ag. [6]

5.5 Vitrification and Tg evolution (why Tg is used as a cure index)

As cure progresses, Tg increases. The excerpt indicates that after gelation, Tg is often used as an index of degree of cure. [8]

5.5.1 Practical implication

  • If the part temperature is below Tg(a), molecular mobility is restricted and cure can slow (system-dependent).
  • For production, Tg measurements (DMA/DSC) can be used to validate that the cure state is sufficient for service.

5.6 Cure cycle variability and what it changes

Cure cycle variations (temperature, pressure, time) change resin flow prior to cure, the level of cure, and the finished thickness. Thickness variation can affect consolidation and curing uniformity. [1]

Practical drift examples (what to watch in production)

  • Tool temperature offset: faster cure → earlier viscosity rise → incomplete fill, more knit line weakness risk.
  • Lower pressure or delayed pressure build: less void collapse → porosity increase (Ch08).
  • Different charge condition (maturity): shifts rheology curve; can mimic a cure-cycle change even if the press recipe is unchanged.

5.7 Parameter identification workflow (what data you need)

A practical development workflow to build usable kinetics for manufacturing and simulation:

  1. DSC experiments: dynamic scans at multiple heating rates + isothermal holds (as feasible).
  2. Fit a kinetic model: choose a model form and fit f(a,T) parameters.
  3. Validate on a “new” cycle: predict a(t) under a temperature profile not used in fitting.
  4. Embed into process models: couple with viscosity model (Ch04) and heat transfer model (Ch06).

5.8 Minimal modeling set for compression molding engineering

If you cannot build a full kinetics model immediately, the minimum set that still improves robustness:

  • Gelation time as a function of molding temperature (or a proxy based on rheology).
  • “Demold-safe time” mapping (measured via part stiffness or distortion tests).
  • Tg after molding cycle as a quality verification metric (especially for structural/electrical applications). [8]

5.9 What’s next: thermal gradients and exotherm

Kinetics alone do not determine cure; you need the temperature field in the part. The next chapter develops the heat transfer + exotherm framework and explains why thick SMC parts can experience interior temperature overshoot driven by reaction heat. [8]

Process overview: press stages and manufacturing workflow

2.1 End-to-end workflow (manufacturing “map”)

  1. Material preparation: bring SMC to controlled temperature (as required), remove backing films, cut charge patterns.
  2. Tool preparation: verify tool temperature uniformity, cleanliness, vent condition, and release state.
  3. Charge placement: place one or multiple charges into the open heated mold (placement strategy depends on geometry).
  4. Mold closing / forming: the press closes; SMC flows to fill cavities, ribs, bosses; air is expelled through vents/parting line.
  5. Cure / dwell: maintain pressure and temperature until cure reaches a defined state (part-dependent definition).
  6. Open / demold: open mold, eject part, manage handling while hot/green.
  7. Trim / finish / inspect: remove flash, drill/trim, surface finishing/painting, QC inspection.
Why this workflow is sensitive

In real manufacturing, temperature and degree of cure change continuously during the cycle, and many properties evolve with them. Therefore, flow, void suppression, cure completion, residual stress/warpage, and surface quality are all coupled to the thermal/pressure history.

2.2 Minimal “press molding” sequence (control emphasis)

A baseline press molding practice emphasizes placing material in the cavity, using cure monitoring when needed, and curing in the press while monitoring pressure, temperature, and time to ensure the cure cycle parameters are met. This approach is associated with high quality parts and low void content when properly executed. [4]

2.3 A stage-based view of the compression molding cycle

Stage A — Pre-contact (approach)

  • Key risks: heat loss from charge, contamination pickup, misplacement.
  • Key controls: standardized time from film removal to molding; standardized placement pattern.

Stage B — Contact, venting, and early flow

  • Goal: start flow and expel air/volatiles before viscosity rises too much.
  • Key risks: trapped air → porosity; premature viscosity rise → incomplete fill / knit-line weakness.
  • Controls: closing speed profile, vent design/maintenance, charge temperature consistency.

Stage C — Filling and packing

  • Goal: fill features fully and densify/consolidate while resin can still accommodate flow and void collapse.
  • Process signature: press force rises as thickness reduces and resistance to flow increases.

Stage D — Cure under pressure (dwell)

  • Goal: reach target state of cure (degree of cure / Tg proxy) with uniformity through thickness.
  • Key coupling: cure history affects viscosity, shrinkage, and final properties; for thick sections, exotherm can contribute significantly to temperature, further accelerating cure and changing viscosity. [8]

Stage E — Opening, demolding, and immediate post-cure handling

  • Key risks: surface damage and potential integrity issues from demolding or tool cleaning practices. [3]
  • Controls: demold temperature window, release management, ejection strategy, operator work instructions.

2.4 Process parameters: what matters, and why

While the details differ by resin system and part geometry, the following parameters repeatedly govern outcomes:

Temperature history

  • Controls reaction rate and degree of cure evolution.
  • Strongly affects viscosity and therefore flow and void suppression.
  • Nonuniform temperature → nonuniform cure → warpage and property gradients.

Pressure / force history

  • Controls compaction/consolidation and void collapse.
  • Interacts with flow: too low → incomplete fill and porosity; too high → flash and fiber wash/segregation risks.

Time / dwell

  • Defines how far cure progresses before demolding.
  • Insufficient dwell can leave incomplete cure; excessive dwell increases cycle time without benefit.

Flow/viscosity state

  • Processing conditions (time–temperature, vacuum/pressure, flow/viscosity) influence porosity, Tg, and surface finish quality. [3]
  • Charge condition (maturation/storage) shifts viscosity window even before heating.

2.5 Cure-cycle variability and thickness variability (why “recipe drift” causes defects)

Cure cycle variations (temperature, pressure, and time) can change resin flow prior to cure, the level of cure, and the finished thickness. Thickness variation in turn affects consolidation and curing uniformity. [1]

Engineering implication

When you observe a defect (porosity, short shot, warpage, surface pinholes), treat it as a coupled problem: (i) flow/fill, (ii) air removal/void collapse, (iii) cure evolution and thermal gradients. You cannot “fix” porosity by pressure alone if viscosity rises too early; you cannot “fix” short shot by temperature alone if venting and charge placement are wrong.

2.6 What “done curing” means (practical definitions)

  • Demold-safe: sufficient stiffness/green strength to eject without distortion or tearing.
  • Dimension-safe: sufficiently advanced cure that post-demold shrinkage/relaxation is minimized.
  • Performance-safe: degree of cure / Tg is high enough to meet mechanical/electrical/thermal requirements.

In thick structures, exotherm can accelerate cure in the interior, so “surface temperature reached setpoint” does not guarantee uniform degree of cure through thickness. [8]

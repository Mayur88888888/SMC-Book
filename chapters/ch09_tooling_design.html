Tooling design: vents, shutoffs, flash land, ejection

Tooling sets the achievable process window

Many process “fixes” (pressure changes, dwell changes) fail if tooling does not allow proper air evacuation and robust thickness control. Since porosity depends on viscosity/flow and vacuum/pressure scheduling [3], venting features and shutoff design are fundamental—not optional.

9.1 Mold architecture for SMC: what must be designed intentionally

  • Parting line strategy (where flash can exist; where vents can live; where cosmetics matter)
  • Shutoffs (thin features that close early and can seal air paths)
  • Flash land (controls resin/filler bleed and defines how the cavity seals)
  • Venting network (where gas escapes during closing/filling)
  • Ejection system (pins/strippers; cosmetic witness mark management)
  • Thermal system (heating, cooling, and temperature uniformity)

9.2 Parting line strategy (cosmetic + venting + maintenance)

Choose a parting line to balance:

  • Cosmetics: hide the seam/flash where possible; avoid Class-A surfaces.
  • Venting access: allow vents at far fill points and around likely front-meet regions (Ch08).
  • Trim strategy: place flash where it can be trimmed reliably without damaging the part.
  • Maintenance: ensure vents and lands can be cleaned without dismantling the tool excessively.

9.3 Flash land design (why it matters beyond “flash amount”)

The flash land is the region near the parting line that controls how the mold seals and how resin can bleed. It influences:

  • Air evacuation timing: the cavity seals when lands close—this defines how long air can escape.
  • Pressure distribution: too restrictive lands can cause pressure rise before air is removed (porosity risk).
  • Material loss and surface effects: excessive bleed can alter local fiber/filler content and surface quality.

Rule-of-thinking (not a numeric rule)

Treat flash land as a “valve”: it must allow gas out early, then seal robustly for packing and curing. If it seals too early, porosity increases; if it seals too late, flash increases and dimensional control suffers.

9.4 Shutoffs: the frequent hidden cause of trapped air

Shutoffs are thin closing regions used to create holes/slots/windows without side actions. They often close early and can seal vents or isolate pockets. Therefore:

  • Identify shutoffs in the CAD early and map “air trap pockets” they can create.
  • Provide local venting around shutoff traps.
  • Coordinate shutoff closure with closing speed profile and packing (Ch10).

9.5 Venting system design (direct implementation of Ch08)

9.5.1 Vent placement logic

  • Farthest fill points (end-of-fill zones)
  • Likely knit-line regions (flow fronts meet; trapped gas risk)
  • Deep-draw pockets and around bosses/ribs where air can remain trapped
  • Symmetry considerations: avoid biasing flow by venting only one side unless intentionally designed

9.5.2 Vent maintainability

Vents clog over time due to resin bleed, filler buildup, and surface fouling. Since processing parameters influence porosity through flow/viscosity and pressure/vacuum history [3], vent condition becomes a first-order process variable.

Production tip

Treat vent cleaning frequency as part of the control plan. A “good cycle” can gradually become a “bad cycle” if vents are not maintained—even if the press recipe is unchanged.

9.6 Ejection and demolding (tooling must protect surface and integrity)

Demolding and tool cleaning can affect laminate integrity and surface finish (scratches/gouges). [3] Tool design should therefore aim to:

  • minimize required demold force (release strategy + draft + surface finish + ejection layout)
  • avoid concentrated ejection loads on thin sections or cosmetically critical surfaces
  • provide stable part support during ejection (reduce distortion while hot/green)

9.6.1 Witness marks (cosmetic and functional)

  • Pin marks are often unavoidable; place them on non-cosmetic surfaces or in regions later covered/trimmed.
  • For electrical parts, avoid witness marks that create tracking paths or seal failures.

9.7 Thermal system and temperature uniformity provisions

Temperature history strongly influences cure rate and viscosity evolution [2], and processing/cure parameters affect Tg and surface finish. [3] Therefore, tooling must support:

  • Uniform heating across the cavity
  • Sensor locations to measure representative tool temperatures (not just at the platens)
  • Calibration access so temperature readings remain trustworthy over the tool life

9.8 Tooling design review checklist (SMC-focused)

  1. Air trap map: have you identified all pockets created by geometry/shutoffs and planned vents?
  2. Vents: placed at end-of-fill and knit-line-prone regions; cleanable; compatible with cosmetics.
  3. Flash management: flash land seals at the correct stage; trimming is feasible without damage.
  4. Ejection: balanced, avoids thin sections, minimizes cosmetic witness marks.
  5. Thermal: heating zones support uniform temperature; sensor plan exists.
  6. Maintenance: tool can be cleaned without compromising alignment and surface condition.

9.9 What’s next: press mechanics and cycle design

With tooling defined, the press cycle must be designed to exploit venting and avoid premature sealing. The next chapter covers press tonnage, closing speed profiles, dwell strategies, and how cycle variations affect flow, degree of cure, and thickness. [1]

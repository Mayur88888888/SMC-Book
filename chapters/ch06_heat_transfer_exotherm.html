Heat transfer and exotherm: thermal gradients and thick parts

Key coupling (must be understood to avoid surprises)

In thick structures, the heat of reaction may contribute significantly to resin temperature, which in turn affects degree of cure and viscosity. This feedback makes it difficult to predict behavior under new temperature profiles without a model. [2]

6.1 Why temperature uniformity is difficult in compression molding

In SMC compression molding, the mold surfaces are typically heated and drive heat into the charge primarily by conduction. However, temperature uniformity can be compromised by:

  • finite thermal diffusivity (interior lags surface)
  • tool temperature nonuniformity (heater zoning, platen gradients)
  • thermal contact resistance at the tool/SMC interface
  • reaction heat generation (exotherm) inside the composite, especially in thick parts [2]
  • material variability (filler/fiber content affects k, cp, density)

6.2 Governing energy balance (with cure heat)

A standard thermochemical heat transfer model for a curing thermoset composite is: 

rho * cp * dT/dt = div( k * grad(T) ) + rho * Hr * (da/dt)

where:

  • rho is density
  • cp is specific heat
  • k is thermal conductivity (tensor in general)
  • Hr is total heat of reaction per unit mass (or per unit resin mass depending on definition)
  • a is degree of cure, governed by a kinetic model da/dt = f(a,T) (Ch05)

What this equation is telling you (engineering interpretation)

  • The temperature field is driven by both tool heating (conduction) and internal heat generation (exotherm).
  • Because da/dt depends on T, the exotherm term can create positive feedback in thick parts.
  • Since viscosity depends on T and a (Ch04), the thermal field indirectly controls filling and void suppression.

6.3 Boundary conditions in a heated mold

Typical boundary descriptions (conceptual):

  • Prescribed tool surface temperature: mold surfaces held near a setpoint (idealization).
  • Heat flux boundary: if heater power is controlled rather than temperature.
  • Thermal contact resistance: imperfect contact early in closing and at flash/parting features can reduce heat transfer.

6.3.1 Contact resistance is process dependent

In early stages, the SMC may not fully contact the mold surface uniformly due to trapped air, local thickness variations, and evolving pressure. Thus, the boundary heat transfer evolves during the closing/packing stage.

6.4 Thick-part exotherm: why the interior can run hotter than the mold

The excerpt explicitly notes that in thick structures, reaction heat can significantly contribute to resin temperature, affecting both cure and viscosity. [2] This has several consequences:

  • Interior temperature overshoot: centerline temperature may exceed tool surface temperature.
  • Nonuniform degree of cure: faster interior cure can occur compared with near-surface regions (depending on boundary conditions).
  • Property gradients: Tg, shrinkage, and residual stresses can vary through thickness.
  • Defect risk: trapped volatiles can expand more under a hotter interior, aggravating porosity/blister risk (Ch08).

6.5 Why “setpoint temperature” is not the same as “part temperature history”

Tool temperature control ensures a boundary condition, not necessarily the internal temperature history. Because the cure rate depends strongly on temperature and cure state evolves nonlinearly [2], modest tool temperature deviations or part thickness changes can produce large changes in:

  • time to gelation (end of significant flow)
  • time to vitrification (mobility restriction)
  • peak temperature and time at peak (controls side reactions, porosity, degradation risk)

6.6 A minimal “thermal verification” plan for molding trials

Even if you do not yet run full simulations, you can reduce surprises by instrumented trials:

  1. Map tool surface temperatures at multiple points (top/bottom halves).
  2. Embed thermocouples in the part (as allowed) to capture through-thickness thermal lag and any exotherm peak.
  3. Correlate temperature traces with defects (porosity, blistering, surface pinholes, warpage).
  4. Use the data to calibrate a simple 1D conduction + exotherm model (sufficient for many thickness-driven issues).

6.7 Coupling back to viscosity and porosity

The excerpt emphasizes that process parameters and thermal history affect degree of cure and viscosity [2], and that processing conditions influence porosity and Tg. [3] In practice:

  • Hotter regions cure faster → viscosity rises earlier → flow stops earlier → greater risk of incomplete fill locally.
  • Regions with delayed heating remain low-viscosity longer → may over-flow and flash under pressure.
  • Nonuniform cure → nonuniform shrinkage → warpage (Ch14).

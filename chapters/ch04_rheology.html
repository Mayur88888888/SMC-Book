Rheology and viscosity evolution during cure

Core idea

SMC flow depends on an evolving viscosity governed by temperature and cure advancement. Early in the cycle, heat reduces viscosity and promotes flow; as reaction proceeds, viscosity rises rapidly due to cross-link network formation. [2][5]

4.1 Why viscosity control is central in thermoset SMC

In thermoset molding, you must accomplish:

  1. Filling and air evacuation while viscosity is sufficiently low.
  2. Consolidation/void collapse under pressure before the resin becomes too “solid-like.”
  3. Cure completion to reach target properties (often tracked through Tg or degree of cure).

The processing window closes because viscosity increases strongly as the polymer network forms. This is why changes in the cure cycle (temperature/pressure/time) can change resin flow prior to cure and final outcomes. [1]

4.2 Qualitative viscosity evolution during a press cycle

A typical thermoset cycle produces the following pattern:

  • Heat-up / early stage: viscosity decreases with temperature (flow becomes easier).
  • Reaction acceleration: viscosity begins rising as crosslink density increases.
  • Approach to gelation: viscosity becomes very large; flow becomes severely restricted.
  • Post-gel: the material behaves increasingly like a viscoelastic solid.

The excerpted processing text explicitly states that as the reaction progresses, viscosity increases exponentially due to cross-link network formation. [2]

4.3 Viscosity models used in process simulation and control

Many phenomenological viscosity models exist to link viscosity to temperature and cure state. One widely used form is the Castro–Macosko model, chosen in the excerpt for its versatility. [5]

4.3.1 A cure-dependent viscosity form (as given in the excerpt)

A representative functional relationship for viscosity is: [6] 

eta(T, a) = eta0(T) * ( ag / (ag - a) )^(A + B*a)

where:

  • eta(T, a) is viscosity (process apparent viscosity)
  • a is degree of cure (0 to 1)
  • ag is degree of cure at gelation (model parameter)
  • A, B are phenomenological coefficients (fit from data)
  • eta0(T) is a reference viscosity term capturing temperature dependence

4.3.2 Arrhenius temperature dependence for the reference viscosity

The excerpt provides an Arrhenius dependence for the reference viscosity term: [6] 

eta0(T) = C * exp( Ea / (R*T) )

where Ea is an activation energy, R is the universal gas constant, and T is absolute temperature. (The excerpt includes a reference temperature notation; your implementation may use equivalent parameterizations.)

Interpretation for SMC engineers

This structure captures two essential truths: (i) heating reduces viscosity initially (increasing flow), but (ii) as cure advances toward gelation, viscosity rises sharply and effectively freezes the flow. Therefore, if gelation/vitrification occur before filling/impregnation, the result can be incomplete impregnation or incomplete fill. [7]

4.4 Gelation and vitrification (process “phase changes”)

As cure advances, a crosslink network forms. The polymer passes through a gelation phase where it transitions toward viscoelastic behavior. [3] Additionally, the instantaneous glass transition temperature increases as the network grows; vitrification occurs when Tg exceeds the laminate temperature. [7]

  • Before gelation: material can flow and redistribute (most filling should occur here).
  • Near/after gelation: flow becomes limited; pressure mainly drives compaction/void collapse and stress development.
  • After vitrification: mobility is strongly limited; further cure can become diffusion-limited (system-dependent).

The excerpt warns that if gelation or vitrification occur before completion of mold filling and preform impregnation, the laminate will not be fully impregnated. [7]

4.5 Building an SMC “flow window map” (practical method)

Even without full constitutive modeling, you can build a practical process map:

  1. Measure viscosity evolution for your SMC at relevant temperatures (isothermal or ramp).
  2. Determine gelation time/degree of cure proxy (rheology crossover, DMA, or DSC-based criteria).
  3. Define minimum required flow time for your geometry and charge coverage ratio (from trials or simulation).
  4. Set the cycle so required fill/venting finishes with margin before viscosity rise makes flow unstable.

Production-friendly surrogates (when lab rheology is limited)

  • Instrument press: track force–position–time during closing; compare to a “golden curve” baseline (Ch11).
  • Short-shot series: intentionally reduce charge mass to visualize flow paths and meet lines.
  • Part mass/thickness mapping: detect drift in flow and flash formation.

4.6 How rheology links to porosity and surface finish

Processing parameters influence porosity, Tg, and surface finish through their impact on flow/viscosity and cure history. [3] In practice:

  • If viscosity becomes high too early, air cannot escape and voids cannot collapse effectively.
  • If viscosity is too low too long under high pressure, you may increase flash and fiber wash/segregation risks.
  • Surface defects (pinholes, flow marks) often correlate with air management and viscosity state near the surface.
